# Government Borrowing

Governments can borrow money by issuing debt and agreeing to make future payments. Issuing debt obtains financing; it is different from collecting tax revenue.

The basic instrument mechanics are introduced in [Debt Securities](../6.%20Financial%20Instruments/1.%20Non-Derivative%20Instruments/4.%20Debt%20Securities/0.%20Debt%20Securities.md) and [Bonds](../6.%20Financial%20Instruments/1.%20Non-Derivative%20Instruments/4.%20Debt%20Securities/Bonds.md). This note focuses on the government as borrower.

## U.S. Treasury Securities

The U.S. Department of the Treasury is a government department. In investing, "Treasuries" usually means the debt securities it issues to borrow money for the federal government.

Examples include Treasury bills, notes, and bonds. Bills are short-term securities commonly sold below the amount repaid at maturity; notes and bonds pay periodic interest. These are not the only Treasury products: inflation-protected securities, floating-rate notes, and savings bonds have different features.

Marketable Treasury securities can be transferred or sold before maturity. U.S. savings bonds are non-marketable products, so the same trading assumptions do not apply to every form of government borrowing.

Government backing does not mean the resale price cannot fall. Selling before maturity can produce a loss, and inflation can reduce what the money received will buy.
