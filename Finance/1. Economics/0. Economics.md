# Economics

An economy is the set of activities and arrangements through which people make, distribute, exchange and use goods and services. Goods are useful items, such as food. Services are work done for others, such as teaching.

The word "economy" comes from the Ancient Greek word oikonomia. Its first part, oikos, means "house," and its second part, nemein, means "to manage." The most common modern meaning, the economic system of a country or area, did not develop until the 1650s.

Economics studies three questions about these activities:

- How they are organized. Example: a city's buses can be run by the city government or by a private company.
- How people choose between different uses of the same resources, such as time, materials and skills. Example: a farmer with one field must choose whether to plant wheat or corn in it.
- Who benefits and who bears the costs. Example: a new factory gives 200 people jobs, while families living next to it breathe its smoke.

## The Embedded Economy

Economic activity takes place within society, and society depends on the natural world. Example: a bakery needs people to work in it and buy from it (society), and it needs wheat that grows only with soil, sunlight and rain (the natural world). The embedded economy model is a simplified picture of these relationships. Two teaching sources use it. The first is the work of Kate Raworth, an English economist at the University of Oxford. She is known for doughnut economics, a model that judges an economy by whether it meets everyone's essential needs without damaging the natural systems people depend on. The second is the Regenerative Economics textbook, which is published online. "Embedded" means that the economy is part of these larger systems, not separate from them.

The list below breaks these relationships into five steps. Each step depends on the steps before it. The list starts with the physical world and ends with the arrangements that finance deals with:

1. **The physical world.** About 13.8 billion years ago, the universe was in an early hot, dense state. Its expansion and cooling are described by the Big Bang model. Much later, stars and planets formed. Earth formed about 4.5 billion years ago. Every good is made from materials and energy found in this physical world. Example: iron ore is dug from the ground and made into steel.
2. **Life and needs.** People depend on water, food, materials and energy. What nature supplies, what people know how to produce, and who can obtain it all affect how needs are met.
3. **Minds and choices.** People learn, remember, imagine the future and care about different things. Example: one person saves money to buy a house, while another spends it on travel. Consciousness means having experiences, such as feeling hungry. Its origins remain debated. Economics does not need to settle that debate to study how people choose.
4. **Cooperation and rules.** Families, communities, businesses and governments organize how resources are used and shared. Customs, ownership rights and laws shape what people can do and expect from one another. Example: the law records who owns a house, so its owner can sell it and a stranger cannot. Exchange is one way of obtaining things. Sharing and unpaid care also provide goods and services. Example: a parent who cooks dinner for a child provides a meal without being paid.
5. **Arrangements across time.** Someone can provide resources now in return for a promise of repayment later. Example: a bank lends someone \$10,000 today, and the borrower promises to repay \$11,000 in two years. This arrangement has three features. It depends on the borrower keeping the promise. The bank must wait two years for its money. It may not turn out as expected: if the borrower loses their job, the bank may get back less than \$11,000. Finance deals with arrangements like this. It also deals with making payments and with providing money for activities such as starting a business.

The five steps are ordered by which step depends on which, not by when each appeared in history. They do not claim that every society developed in this order.
