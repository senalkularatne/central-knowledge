# Prices

A price is what a buyer gives in exchange for one unit of a good or service. Every [exchange](2.%20Exchange%20and%20Specialization.md) sets a price, even a swap of bread for shoes. Because resources are [scarce](1.%20Scarcity%20and%20Choice.md), prices also show how much people want something compared with how much of it is available.

## What a Price Is

A price can be stated in two ways:

- A nominal price is stated in money, such as \$2 for a loaf of bread.
- A relative price is stated in another good or service. If a loaf costs \$2 and a pair of shoes costs \$60, the relative price of the shoes is 30 loaves.

In barter, every price is a relative price. Money lets every price be stated in the same unit, which makes prices easy to compare; see [Money and Banking](../../2.%20Money%20and%20Banking/0.%20Money%20and%20Banking.md).

When economists talk about prices, they usually mean relative prices. A relative price shows the [opportunity cost](1.%20Scarcity%20and%20Choice.md#trade-offs-and-opportunity-cost) of a purchase: the shoes cost the 30 loaves that could have been bought instead. If all nominal prices rose by the same percentage, relative prices would not change. A rise in the general level of prices is called [inflation](../../2.%20Money%20and%20Banking/0.%20Money%20and%20Banking.md#inflation).

## Demand

Demand is the amount of a good or service that buyers are willing and able to purchase at each possible price. Both are needed: wanting something without being able to pay for it is not demand. Demand covers the whole range of prices. The amount buyers would purchase at one particular price is the quantity demanded.

The law of demand says that a higher price typically leads to a lower quantity demanded. At a higher price, buyers find ways to use less or switch to something else. The law assumes that everything else affecting demand stays the same. Economists call this assumption "other things being equal," or ceteris paribus in Latin.

## Supply

Supply is the amount of a good or service that producers are willing to sell at each possible price. The amount they would sell at one particular price is the quantity supplied.

The law of supply says that a higher price typically leads to a higher quantity supplied, other things being equal. A higher price makes producing more profitable, so producers expand output.

## Equilibrium

Equilibrium is the price at which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. At that price, the plans of buyers and sellers match. The word means balance.

| Price per gallon | Quantity demanded | Quantity supplied | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| \$1.00 | 800 | 500 | Shortage of 300 |
| \$1.40 | 600 | 600 | Equilibrium |
| \$1.80 | 500 | 680 | Surplus of 180 |

The table is a textbook example for gasoline from OpenStax, with quantities in millions of gallons.

Above the equilibrium price, sellers want to sell more than buyers want to buy. This is a surplus, also called excess supply. Unsold goods pile up, so sellers cut prices. Below the equilibrium price, buyers want more than sellers offer. This is a shortage, also called excess demand. Sellers can raise prices and still sell everything they have. In either case, these pressures push the price toward equilibrium.

## What Moves Demand and Supply

A change in a good's own price changes the quantity demanded and the quantity supplied. It does not change demand or supply. A change in anything else that affects buyers or producers shifts demand or supply, meaning a different quantity at every price.

Demand shifts with changes in the following. These are examples, not a complete list.

- Tastes and preferences.
- The size and makeup of the population.
- Income. For most goods, demand rises when income rises; these are normal goods. For some, such as generic-brand groceries, demand falls because people switch to name brands; these are inferior goods.
- Prices of related goods. A substitute is used in place of another good, such as tablets in place of laptops; a lower price for one lowers demand for the other. Complements are used together, such as cereal and milk; a lower price for one raises demand for the other.
- Expectations about future prices, income and other conditions.

Supply shifts with changes in the following. These are examples, not a complete list.

- Prices of inputs, such as labor and raw materials.
- Natural conditions, such as weather.
- Technology.
- Government taxes, regulations and subsidies.

When only one side changes, the new equilibrium moves as follows:

| Change | Price | Quantity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Demand rises | Rises | Rises |
| Demand falls | Falls | Falls |
| Supply rises | Falls | Rises |
| Supply falls | Rises | Falls |

For example, a drought reduces the supply of wheat, so the price of wheat rises and the quantity sold falls.

## Prices as Signals

The knowledge needed to use resources well is spread across many people, and no one has all of it. In 1945 the economist Friedrich Hayek argued that prices pass on the part that matters. His example was tin. If tin becomes scarcer, because of a new use or a lost source, users do not need to know which. Its rising price tells them to use less. The effect spreads to substitutes for tin and to things made from it.

Hayek called the price system "a mechanism for communicating information." He added that it does this "less perfectly as prices grow more rigid," meaning as prices become slower to change.

Prices are also [incentives](1.%20Scarcity%20and%20Choice.md#deciding-at-the-margin). A higher price rewards producers for making more and leads buyers to use less.

## Price and Value

Value has more than one meaning. In *The Wealth of Nations* (1776), Adam Smith separated "value in use," meaning how useful something is, from "value in exchange," meaning how much of other goods it can buy. He wrote: "Nothing is more useful than water; but it will purchase scarce anything; scarce anything can be had in exchange for it. A diamond, on the contrary, has scarce any value in use: but a very great quantity of other goods may frequently be had in exchange for it."

This came to be called the paradox of value, or the diamond-water paradox. In the late nineteenth century, economists explained it with marginal utility. Water gives far more total utility than diamonds. But water is plentiful, so one more bucket adds little; this is [diminishing marginal utility](1.%20Scarcity%20and%20Choice.md#deciding-at-the-margin). Diamonds are scarce, so one more adds a lot. What people will pay depends on the value of one more unit, not the value of the whole supply.

The same distinction matters in investing. In his 2008 letter to Berkshire Hathaway shareholders, Warren Buffett wrote: "Long ago, Ben Graham taught me that 'Price is what you pay; value is what you get.'" Estimating what an investment is worth is covered in [Investment Analysis](../../12.%20Investment%20Analysis/0.%20Investment%20Analysis.md).

## Limits

Equilibrium is a tendency, not a guarantee. Real markets take time to adjust, and some prices change rarely. Governments sometimes set legal maximum or minimum prices. A maximum set below equilibrium causes shortages, and a minimum set above it causes surpluses.

Demand counts only what buyers are able to pay. A need that someone cannot pay for does not appear in demand, so prices reflect the wants of people who have money.
