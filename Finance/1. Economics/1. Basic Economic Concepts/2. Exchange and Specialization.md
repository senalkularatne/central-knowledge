# Exchange and Specialization

Exchange means giving something and receiving something in return. Specialization means a person or business focuses on a narrow range of work and obtains everything else from others. The two depend on each other: a person can specialize only if they can obtain the other things they need.

## Forms of Exchange

Barter is a direct swap of goods or services without using money. A swap happens only if each side wants what the other offers. A baker who wants shoes must find a shoemaker who wants bread. This requirement is called the double coincidence of wants. Money, explained later in [Money and Banking](../../2.%20Money%20and%20Banking/0.%20Money%20and%20Banking.md), removes it: the baker can sell bread to anyone and buy shoes from anyone.

The barter example shows a problem that money solves. It is not evidence that societies once ran on barter alone before money existed. The anthropologist Caroline Humphrey wrote in 1985 that "no example of a barter economy, pure and simple, has ever been described." Barter does happen, for example in trade between distant communities and where money is scarce or unreliable.

Reciprocity is giving and receiving within a relationship, such as among family, friends or a community, without setting a price. The return may come much later, in a different form, or to someone else. Not every exchange is a sale.

An exchange can also be split across time: one side gives now and the other promises to give later. These promises are explained in [Obligations and Claims](../../3.%20Assets%20and%20Liabilities/0.%20Assets%20and%20Liabilities.md#obligations-and-claims).

## Division of Labor

The division of labor means splitting the production of something into tasks done by different workers. In *The Wealth of Nations* (1776), Adam Smith described a pin workshop where making a pin was divided into about 18 separate tasks. He reported that 10 workers dividing the tasks could make upwards of 48,000 pins a day, while each working alone could not have made 20, perhaps not even one.

Smith gave three reasons dividing the work raises production:

1. Workers who repeat one task become more skilled at it.
2. Workers save the time lost switching from one kind of work to another.
3. Focusing on one task leads people to invent machines that do more of the work.

Economists today add a related reason. Producing more of one thing can lower the cost of making each unit, for example by using an assembly line. This is called economies of scale.

## Comparative Advantage

Someone has an absolute advantage in a task if they can produce more of it with the same resources. Someone has a comparative advantage in a task if they can do it at a lower [opportunity cost](1.%20Scarcity%20and%20Choice.md#trade-offs-and-opportunity-cost), meaning they give up less to do it.

For example, a lawyer who types faster than her assistant has an absolute advantage in typing. She still gains by letting the assistant type: every hour she spends typing is an hour not spent on legal work, which earns far more. Her opportunity cost of typing is higher, so the assistant has the comparative advantage in typing.

When each side specializes in what it has a comparative advantage in and they trade, total production rises. This works even when one side is better at everything. The same reasoning applies to people, businesses and countries.

## Gains and Their Limits

When an exchange is voluntary, each side takes part because it expects to value what it receives more than what it gives. That is how both sides can gain from the same exchange.

The gains are not shared evenly. As trade between countries grows, jobs shift toward industries with a comparative advantage and away from others. Workers in industries facing new competition can see their wages fall or lose their jobs, even when average wages rise.

Specialization also creates dependence. Someone who produces only one thing relies on exchange for everything else, so a disruption to trade affects them more.

## Rules That Make Exchange Dependable

Exchange works only if people can rely on it. Institutions are the rules people create to shape how they deal with one another:

- Informal rules: customs, traditions, codes of conduct, and social penalties such as losing one's reputation.
- Formal rules: constitutions, laws and property rights.

Property rights are the recognized rights to use something, to keep others from using it, and to sell or give it away. A person can reliably trade only what others accept that person owns.

Rules reduce uncertainty. Each side can better predict what the other will do, and what happens if someone breaks the deal. By setting rewards and penalties, rules also act as [incentives](1.%20Scarcity%20and%20Choice.md#deciding-at-the-margin).

## Early Evidence

As of a 2018 study, the earliest known evidence of moving materials over long distances comes from Olorgesailie, Kenya, about 295,000 to 320,000 years ago. People there made tools from obsidian, a volcanic glass, most of it brought from 25 to 50 kilometers away and a few pieces from farther. The researchers say this suggests networks of exchange or of travel to collect materials; the evidence does not show which.
