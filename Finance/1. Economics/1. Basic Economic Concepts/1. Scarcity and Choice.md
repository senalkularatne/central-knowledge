# Scarcity and Choice

Scarcity means there are not enough resources to provide everything people would like to use them for. Using a resource one way means it cannot be used another way, so people, businesses and governments must choose. Example: a family has \$500 left after paying its bills this month. If it spends the \$500 on a new phone, it cannot also spend it on a weekend trip.

## Resources

Resources are what people use to produce goods and services. Economists call them factors of production and group them into four kinds:

- Land: anything that comes from nature, such as ground, water, oil, minerals and forests. Raw materials belong here.
- Labor: the work people do, whether physical or mental.
- Capital: machines, tools and buildings used to produce other goods and services, such as a delivery van or a computer.
- Entrepreneurship: organizing land, labor and capital to produce something, in the hope of making a profit. Profit is the money a business has left after paying its costs. Example: a bakery that sells \$10,000 of bread in a month and pays \$8,000 for flour, wages and rent makes a \$2,000 profit.

In economics, capital means physical things like these, not money. Money used to start or run a business is called financial capital. In finance, "capital" usually means financial capital. Example: when a company "raises \$5 million in capital," it collects \$5 million of money, not machines.

Time is also limited. Everyone has 24 hours in a day to divide between work, rest and everything else.

## Trade-Offs and Opportunity Cost

A trade-off means getting more of one thing requires accepting less of another. Example: a city that spends more of its budget on roads has less left to spend on parks.

The opportunity cost of a choice is the value of the next best alternative given up. For example, an hour spent studying cannot also be spent at a job. If that job pays \$20 an hour, the \$20 not earned is part of the cost of studying for that hour.

Opportunity cost is not always the money paid. Going to college costs tuition and books, and also the earnings given up by studying instead of working.

## Deciding at the Margin

Marginal means one more or one less. Most choices are not all or nothing. They are about a little more or a little less of something. Marginal analysis compares the extra benefit of a small change with its extra cost. Example: a student deciding whether to study one more hour compares how much that hour would raise her grade with the \$20 she could earn at her job in that hour.

Economists call the satisfaction or benefit a person gets from a good or service utility. Utility is personal. The same meal gives more utility to someone who is hungry than to someone who has just eaten. Utility is also not the same as price. Example: a glass of tap water costs almost nothing but matters a great deal to someone who is thirsty. A diamond ring can cost thousands of dollars but matter little to someone who does not wear jewelry.

The extra utility from each additional unit usually falls as a person has more of the same thing. The first slice of pizza is usually more satisfying than the sixth. This is the law of diminishing marginal utility. It is why people usually choose a mix of things instead of only one. Example: someone with \$15 for lunch usually buys a sandwich and a drink, not three sandwiches.

A sunk cost is a cost already paid that cannot be recovered. It is the same whatever is chosen next, so it should not affect the next choice. Someone who paid for a movie that turns out to be terrible should decide whether to stay based on what the next 90 minutes are worth, not on the ticket already bought.

An incentive is a reward or penalty that changes the benefit or cost of a choice. Because choices depend on extra benefits and costs, changing them can change what people choose. Example: a store that charges 10 cents for each plastic bag gives shoppers a reason to bring their own bags.

## Choices for a Whole Society

A society faces the same problem. A production possibilities frontier shows the combinations of two goods, such as healthcare and education, that a society can produce with all its resources. It is called a frontier because it is the boundary between what the society can produce and what it cannot. Producing more of one means producing less of the other. A combination inside the frontier means some resources are unused or wasted. A combination beyond it cannot currently be produced.

The opportunity cost usually rises as a society produces more of one good, because resources are not equally suited to every use. Example: suppose a society keeps moving doctors into teaching. The first doctors to move are the ones best suited to teaching, so the society gains a lot of education and loses little healthcare. The last doctors to move are the ones best suited to medicine, such as skilled surgeons, so the society gains little education and loses a lot of healthcare. This is the law of increasing opportunity cost.

## Limits

Opportunity cost and marginal analysis describe how a person could weigh a choice. People do not always choose this way. Emotions, habits and putting things off also affect decisions. Behavioral economics is the study of these patterns. For example, some new employees put off joining a retirement savings plan because they procrastinate or feel overwhelmed by the choices.

Scarcity explains why choices must be made. It does not by itself explain who goes without. That also depends on how resources and income are divided among people.
