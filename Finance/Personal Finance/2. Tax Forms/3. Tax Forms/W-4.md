# W-4

## What is it?

The W-4 is a form you give to your employer that tells them how much **federal income tax** to withhold from each paycheck. It does NOT affect Social Security, Medicare, or state taxes — those are calculated automatically.

You fill it out when you start a new job, but you can update it **anytime** through your payroll system (e.g., Dayforce). Changes take effect on future paychecks.

---

## Why is it important?

Your employer doesn't know your full tax situation. They don't know if you have a second job, a spouse who works, dependents, or other deductions. The W-4 is how you tell them so they can estimate your withholding more accurately.

If your W-4 is wrong, one of two things happens:

- **Too little withheld** → you owe money when you file your tax return (and possibly penalties)
- **Too much withheld** → you get a refund, but that means you gave the government a free loan all year instead of having that money in your paycheck

The goal is to get as close to \$0 owed / \$0 refund as possible.

---

## How withholding works

Every pay period, your employer looks at:
1. Your W-4 settings
2. How much you earned that paycheck
3. IRS withholding tables

They calculate an estimate of your annual income based on that one paycheck, figure out the tax on it, and withhold that fraction. This is just an **estimate** — it can be wrong, especially if:

- Your income changes during the year (raise, bonus, RSU vesting)
- You have income from other sources with no withholding
- Your W-4 doesn't reflect your actual situation

### Why withholding can't be exact

You can't know the exact amount to withhold in advance because your final income for the year isn't known until the year is over. Raises, bonuses, RSU vesting amounts, and overtime are all unpredictable. The best tool to get close is the [IRS Tax Withholding Estimator](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/tax-withholding-estimator) — plug in your year-to-date pay stubs and it calculates what to put in Step 4(c) based on what's already been withheld and what's projected for the rest of the year.

Another approach: after you file your tax return, take what you owed and divide by the number of pay periods in the year. For example, if you owed \$1,500 and get paid biweekly (26 paychecks), put \$58 in Step 4(c). This uses last year as a baseline — not exact, but close enough that you'd owe very little or get a small refund.

## The 5 steps of the W-4

The current W-4 (redesigned in 2020) has 5 steps. You only need to complete Steps 1 and 5 — the rest are optional adjustments.

### Step 1 — Personal information (required)

- Your name, address, Social Security number
- Your **filing status**: Single, Married Filing Jointly, or Head of Household
- Filing status matters because it determines which tax brackets and standard deduction apply to you (see [2. Income Tax.md](../1.%20How%20Taxes%20Work/2.%20Income%20Tax.md))

### Step 2 — Multiple jobs or spouse works (optional)

- Only fill this out if you have more than one job at the same time, or if you're married filing jointly and your spouse also works
- Why it matters: each employer withholds as if their paycheck is your **only** income. If you have two jobs each paying \$70,000, each employer withholds as if you make \$70,000 (lower bracket). But your actual income is \$140,000 (higher bracket). This gap causes you to owe at tax time
- Options:
  - Use the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator (https://www.irs.gov/individuals/tax-withholding-estimator)
  - Check the box in 2(c) for a simpler but less accurate adjustment
  - Use the worksheet on page 3 of the W-4

### Step 3 — Claim dependents (optional)

- If you have qualifying children or other dependents, you enter tax credits here
- Each qualifying child under 17 = \$2,000 credit
- Other dependents = \$500 credit
- This **reduces** how much is withheld because credits reduce your final tax bill dollar-for-dollar

### Step 4 — Other adjustments (optional)

This step has three parts:

#### 4(a) — Other income

- Enter income you expect to receive that won't have withholding — interest, dividends, side income, retirement distributions
- This tells your employer to withhold extra to cover the tax on that additional income

#### 4(b) — Deductions

- If you plan to itemize deductions (instead of taking the standard deduction), and your itemized deductions will be more than the standard deduction, enter the difference here
- This **reduces** withholding because your taxable income will be lower than the standard assumption
- Most people skip this because the standard deduction is usually better

#### 4(c) — Extra withholding

- A specific dollar amount you want withheld from **every paycheck** on top of the normal calculation
- Use this if you know you'll owe — for example, if you consistently owe at tax time, adding \$50 or \$100 per paycheck here can fix that

### Step 5 — Sign and date (required)

- Just your signature. The form is not valid without it

## Common scenarios

### You're single with one W-2 job and no dependents

- Fill out Steps 1 and 5 only
- Leave everything else blank
- The default withholding should be close to accurate

### You consistently owe at tax time

- Use Step 4(c) to add extra withholding per paycheck
- To figure out how much: take what you owed last year, divide by the number of paychecks per year
- Example: you owed \$1,200 last year and get paid biweekly (26 paychecks). Add \$46 to Step 4(c)

### You get a large refund every year

- Your W-4 is over-withholding. You could reduce it by:
  - Making sure your filing status in Step 1 is correct
  - Adding deductions in Step 4(b) if you itemize
  - Adjusting claims in Step 3

### You have RSUs or bonuses

- RSUs and bonuses are often withheld at a flat 22% supplemental rate, regardless of your actual tax bracket
- If your marginal bracket is higher than 22% (e.g., 24%), you'll be under-withheld on that income
- Fix: add extra withholding in Step 4(c) to make up the difference

## Managing your W-4

### Is submitting a W-4 mandatory?

- **Yes, when you start a new job.** Your employer needs it to know how much to withhold.
- **No, you don't have to resubmit it.** Once it's on file, your employer keeps using those settings until you change them. There's no annual requirement or deadline to update it.
- **Updating is optional but recommended** whenever your tax situation changes (marriage, new dependents, you owed a lot, you got a big refund, etc.)

### When is the best time to update your W-4?

- **Best: early January** — the start of the new tax year (see [1. What are Taxes.md](../1.%20How%20Taxes%20Work/1.%20What%20are%20Taxes.md)). The new withholding applies to every paycheck for the full year, giving the most even and accurate result
- **Good: right after filing taxes (April)** — you know exactly what you owed or got back from the prior year, so you can calculate 4(c) accurately. You've only missed ~3 months of paychecks
- **If you update mid-year**: divide what you owe by the **remaining** pay periods, not all 26. For example, if you update in April with 20 paychecks left: \$1,500 ÷ 20 = \$75/paycheck. Then in January of the next year, drop it to the full-year number (\$1,500 ÷ 26 = \$58/paycheck)
- **Anytime something changes**: got married, had a kid, got a big raise, started a second job — update your W-4 promptly

### How to update your W-4

- You update it through your employer's payroll system — not the IRS
- It only affects **future** paychecks, not past ones
- You can update it as many times as you want throughout the year
- There's no penalty for changing it

### Where to find your W-4

#### Blank form (for reference)

- [IRS W-4 form](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-w-4)

#### To view or update yours in Dayforce

The W-4 isn't a PDF you download — it's a settings page in your payroll system. In Dayforce:

1. Go to **Profile** (from the home page)
2. Click **Forms**
3. Click **Federal W4 - 2026**

Dayforce breaks the W-4 into individual fields (filing status, dependents, extra withholding, etc.) that match the steps above. You won't see an actual "W-4 form" — just the fields.

If the form is grayed out or you can't edit it, your employer may require you to go through HR. Email HR/PeopleCenter and ask them to update your federal withholding.

#### Ohio state withholding (IT-4)

In the same Forms section, you'll also see **Province/State Tax Form** — this is the Ohio IT-4, which is the state equivalent of the W-4. Same concept but for Ohio state income tax withholding.

#### IRS Tax Withholding Estimator

- https://www.irs.gov/individuals/tax-withholding-estimator
- Use this to calculate the most accurate number for Step 4(c)
- Have your most recent pay stub handy when using it

## Filing your tax return

The W-4 controls withholding, but you still need to **file a tax return** every year by April 15. These are separate things:

- **W-4**: tells your employer how much to withhold (set it and forget it until you need to change)
- **Tax return**: the annual filing where you report income and settle up with the IRS

### What happens if you don't file?

- **Failure-to-file penalty**: 5% of unpaid taxes for each month you're late, up to 25% of the amount owed
- **Failure-to-pay penalty**: 0.5% of unpaid taxes per month, up to 25%. This applies even if you file on time but don't pay what you owe
- **Interest**: the IRS charges interest on unpaid taxes, compounding daily
- **If you're owed a refund**: no penalty for filing late, but you lose the refund if you wait more than 3 years
- **Extension**: you can file Form 4868 to get an automatic extension until October 15, but you must still **pay** what you owe by April 15 to avoid penalties

### Best time to file your tax return

- **As early as possible (late January–February)**: you get your refund fastest, and you have your W-2 in hand. Also reduces risk of identity theft (someone filing a fake return with your SSN before you)
- **By April 15**: the deadline. No penalties if you file and pay by this date
- **If you can't finish by April 15**: file an extension (Form 4868) for free. Gives you until October 15 to file. But estimate and pay what you owe by April 15 to avoid penalties

## W-4 vs W-2

These are different forms that are easy to confuse:

| | W-4 | W-2 |
|---|-----|-----|
| **What** | Instructions you give your employer | Report your employer gives you |
| **When** | When you start a job (or anytime you update it) | January–February, after the year ends |
| **Purpose** | Controls how much tax is withheld per paycheck | Summarizes what you earned and what was withheld |
| **Who fills it out** | You | Your employer |
| **Filed with IRS?** | No — stays with your employer | Yes — you use it to file your tax return |
