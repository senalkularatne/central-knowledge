# Assets and Liabilities

An asset is something of economic value that a person or organization owns or controls. A liability is an obligation owed to someone else. Whether something is an asset or a liability depends on whose side you look from: a loan is an asset of the lender and a liability of the borrower.

## Obligations and Claims

A promise is an assurance that someone will do something, or will not do it. A promise can create an obligation: a duty to do or not do something.

A contract is an agreement that creates obligations enforceable by law. A party that breaks one may be ordered to pay damages, meaning money to make up for the loss. Not every promise is a contract; the law sets conditions, and the details vary between places.

An obligation owed to someone has two sides. If one person has a duty to pay, another has the right to be paid. A right to receive something from someone else is a claim. One person's claim is another person's obligation.

The person who owes is the debtor, and the person who is owed is the creditor. What the debtor owes is a debt.

Credit is providing money, goods or services now in the expectation of payment later. The word comes from the Latin *credere*, "to trust, entrust, believe": a creditor trusts the debtor to pay.

For example, a farmer lends a neighbor 100 kilograms of grain in spring, and the neighbor promises to return 110 kilograms after the harvest. The neighbor is the debtor and has an obligation. The farmer is the creditor and has a claim. The extra 10 kilograms is interest, a payment for the use of what was borrowed; interest on money is explained in [Money and Banking](../2.%20Money%20and%20Banking/0.%20Money%20and%20Banking.md#interest-and-compounding).

A claim to be paid is an asset of the creditor, and the obligation to pay is a liability of the debtor. The loan agreement itself is explained later under Financial Instruments.
