# Single Payments

## Future Value

Future value is the amount that a sum today grows into by a later date at a given interest rate.

For example, at a hypothetical rate of 4% a year, \$1,000 deposited today grows like this:

| After | Interest earned that year | Balance |
|---|---|---|
| 1 year | \$40.00 | \$1,040.00 |
| 2 years | \$41.60 | \$1,081.60 |

The second year's interest is \$1.60 more than the first year's because it is also earned on the first year's \$40. This is compounding. Over longer periods the effect grows: at the same rate, \$1,000 becomes about \$1,480 after 10 years and about \$7,107 after 50 years.

Each year, the balance is multiplied by 1 plus the rate:

`Future value = Present value × (1 + r)^n`

Here r is the interest rate per period, written as a decimal (4% is 0.04), and n is the number of periods.

A quick estimate, the rule of 72, divides 72 by the rate in percent to approximate how many years it takes an amount to double. At 4%, that is 72 ÷ 4 = 18 years. The exact answer is about 17.7 years, so the rule is only an approximation.

## Present Value and Discounting

Present value is what an amount paid in the future is worth today at a given interest rate. Finding it is called discounting, the reverse of compounding. The rate used is called the discount rate.

`Present value = Future value ÷ (1 + r)^n`

For example, at 4% a year, \$1,081.60 paid two years from now has a present value of \$1,000, because \$1,000 deposited today would grow into exactly that amount. At the same rate, \$1,000 paid two years from now is worth about \$924.56 today.

The higher the discount rate, or the longer the wait, the smaller the present value.

## Comparing Payments

Suppose the choice is between \$1,000 today and \$1,050 in one year.

| Interest rate | \$1,000 today grows to | Present value of \$1,050 | Worth more |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4% | \$1,040.00 | \$1,009.62 | \$1,050 in one year |
| 6% | \$1,060.00 | \$990.57 | \$1,000 today |

Which payment is worth more depends on the interest rate. A series of payments can be compared the same way, by moving each payment to the same date and adding them up.

## Limits

- The answer depends on the interest rate assumed. A different rate gives a different answer.
- Money today is not automatically worth more. In these calculations it comes out ahead because it can earn interest. OpenStax notes that present value is always less than future value "unless the interest rate is negative."
- Future value counts money, not what the money will buy. If prices rise, a larger future amount may still buy less; see [Inflation](../2.%20Money%20and%20Banking/0.%20Money%20and%20Banking.md#inflation).
- The calculations assume the future payment will be made. A promise can be broken; see [Loans](../6.%20Financial%20Instruments/1.%20Non-Derivative%20Instruments/3.%20Loans.md#two-sides-of-the-agreement). How uncertainty affects the rate people ask for is covered in [Risk and Return](../5.%20Risk%20and%20Return/0.%20Risk%20and%20Return.md).
