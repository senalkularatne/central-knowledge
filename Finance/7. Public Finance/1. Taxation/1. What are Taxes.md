# What are Taxes

Taxes are mandatory payments to the government (federal, state, local) that fund public services. There are many different types — they are NOT all the same thing.

---

## How are taxes collected?

- All tax rates are **annual** — they describe what you owe for the entire year. Some taxes like federal income tax use a tiered system where different portions of your income are taxed at different rates (explained in [0. Income Tax.md](2.%20Types%20of%20Tax/Income%20Tax/0.%20Income%20Tax.md)
- But you don't pay once a year. Your employer **withholds** taxes from every paycheck throughout the year as an estimate
- At year end, you file a tax return to compare what was withheld vs what you actually owe
- Example: Medicare is 1.45% of all wages. If you earn \$100,000/year and are paid biweekly, each paycheck your employer withholds 1.45% of that paycheck's wages (~\$56). Over 26 paychecks that totals ~\$1,450 — which is 1.45% of \$100,000

## Types of taxes

### 1. Income Taxes

Taxes on money you earn from working or other sources.

#### Federal Income Tax
- The biggest one for most people — funds the federal government
- Uses progressive brackets (see [0. Income Tax.md](2.%20Types%20of%20Tax/Income%20Tax/0.%20Income%20Tax.md))
- Amount withheld from your paycheck depends on your W-4 settings
- W-2 Box 2 shows how much was withheld for the year
- You reconcile what you owe vs what was withheld when you file your tax return. If too little was withheld, you owe. If too much, you get a refund

#### State Income Tax
- Varies by state — some states have no income tax (FL, TX, WA, NV, TN, WY, SD, AK, NH)
- Ohio uses brackets from 0% to 3.5%
- W-2 Box 17 shows how much was withheld
- Filed on a separate state tax return

#### Local/City Income Tax
- Some cities/counties add their own tax on top of state
- Common in Ohio — many cities charge 1-2.5%
- May or may not appear on your W-2

### 2. Payroll Taxes (FICA)

Flat-rate taxes on wages that fund specific programs. These are separate from income tax.

#### Social Security Tax (OASDI)
- Flat rate: **6.2%** of your wages
- Wage cap: only the first **\$176,100** (2025) is taxed — anything above is not subject to Social Security tax
- Your employer pays a matching 6.2% on top of yours
- W-2 Box 4 shows your share withheld
- Funds Social Security retirement/disability benefits

#### Medicare Tax
- Flat rate: **1.45%** of all wages (no cap)
- Additional **0.9%** on wages above \$200,000 (single) — this extra portion has no employer match
- Your employer pays a matching 1.45%
- W-2 Box 6 shows your share withheld
- Funds Medicare health coverage (age 65+)

### 3. Sales Tax
- Tax on goods and services you buy
- Set by state and local governments — ranges from 0% to 10%+
- Ohio state rate is 5.75%, plus local additions
- No federal sales tax in the US
- You pay this at the register — it's not on your paycheck or tax return (usually)

### 4. Property Tax
- Tax on real estate (home, land) you own
- Assessed by county/local government based on property value
- Typically 1-2% of assessed value per year
- Funds local schools, fire, police, infrastructure
- Paid annually or through your mortgage escrow
- Can be deducted on your federal return if you itemize (up to \$10,000 SALT cap)

### 5. Capital Gains Tax
- Tax on profit from selling investments (stocks, real estate, crypto, etc.)
- **Short-term** (held < 1 year): taxed at your ordinary income tax rate
- **Long-term** (held ≥ 1 year): taxed at lower rates — 0%, 15%, or 20% depending on income
- You only pay when you **sell** at a profit — unrealized gains are not taxed
- See `5. Tax Strategy/Capital Gains.md` for more detail

### 6. Estate & Gift Tax
- **Estate tax**: tax on the total value of a deceased person's assets before they're passed on. Federal exemption is ~\$13.99 million (2025) — most people don't owe this
- **Gift tax**: tax on giving someone more than \$19,000 (2025) in a year. The giver pays, not the receiver. Doesn't apply until you exceed the lifetime exemption (~\$13.99 million)
- Most people will never deal with either of these

### 7. Excise Tax
- Tax on specific goods: gasoline, alcohol, tobacco, airline tickets
- Usually built into the price — you don't see it as a separate line item
- Federal gas tax: 18.4 cents/gallon

### 8. Employer-Paid Taxes

Your employer pays additional taxes on top of your wages. These are not deducted from your paycheck — your employer pays them separately. You'll never see these on your pay stub, but they do show up on your total compensation statement.

| Tax | What it is |
|-----|-----------|
| Federal Unemployment (FUTA) | Funds unemployment insurance. 6% on first \$7,000 of wages, but effectively 0.6% after credits |
| State Unemployment (SUTA) | State-level unemployment insurance. Rate varies by employer |
| Workers Compensation | Insurance for workplace injuries. Rate varies by industry |

## The big picture — where your paycheck goes

```
Gross pay
− Federal income tax (brackets-based, W-4 dependent)
− Social Security tax (6.2% flat, up to wage cap)
− Medicare tax (1.45% flat, no cap)
− State income tax (varies by state)
− Local income tax (varies by city)
− Pre-tax deductions (health insurance, 401k, HSA — if any)
= Net pay (what hits your bank account)
```

## What is a tax year?

A tax year is the 12-month period your income and taxes are calculated over. For most people, the tax year is the **calendar year** — January 1 through December 31.

Everything resets on January 1:

- Your income starts over at \$0 for bracket purposes
- Tax bracket thresholds reset
- Withholding calculations start fresh
- Contribution limits for accounts like 401(k), IRA, and HSA reset

When people say "the 2025 tax year," they mean income earned from January 1, 2025 through December 31, 2025. You **file** the tax return for that year in the following year (by April 15, 2026).

This is why early January is the best time to update your W-4 — the new withholding applies to every paycheck for the full year, giving the most even and accurate result.

---

## When do you deal with taxes?

- **Every paycheck** — income + payroll taxes withheld automatically based on your W-4
- **Every purchase** — sales tax added at the register
- **January–February** — employer sends you a W-2 summarizing the year
- **By April 15** — file your federal and state tax returns. Compare what was withheld vs what you actually owe
- **If you owe** — pay by April 15 to avoid penalties, even if you file an extension
- **If you own property** — property tax due dates vary by county (often twice a year)
